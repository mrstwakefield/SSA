Smallest space analysis, step by step

A hands-on walk through how investigative psychologists turn a pile of crime reports into a map of offending styles, and how that map feeds into geographical profiling.

Everything below runs on simulated burglary data, generated fresh in your browser. The map in step 3 is computed from the data, not drawn by hand, so changing the sample changes the result. Use the controls here to change how many cases are in the sample.

1

The case file

Analysts read police reports and code each offence for whether a behaviour was present or absent. Each column below is one burglary, and each row is one behaviour. A dot means the behaviour happened. Only the first 20 cases are shown.

In real life, nobody knows which "style" each burglar has. That's the thing SSA is trying to uncover. Tick the box to reveal the hidden style used to simulate each case.

2

Counting what goes together

Next, every pair of behaviours gets an association score. The usual choice is Jaccard's coefficient. It counts how many cases have both behaviours, then divides by how many cases have at least one of them. Cases with neither are ignored, because the absence of a behaviour in a police report is often just missing information.

3

The map

With 12 behaviours there are 66 pairs, which is far too many numbers to read. SSA places the behaviours as points on a plane so that pairs with high association scores end up close together. This page uses classical multidimensional scaling, a close relative of Guttman's SSA, to find those positions. Points that land almost on top of each other are nudged apart slightly so the labels stay readable.

Click a point to see its links. Then turn on the themes and regions and compare them with the layout.

4

Reading the map

Researchers look for two patterns.

  • Centre versus edge. Behaviours almost every offender shows, like searching rooms, co-occur with everything, so they get pulled into the middle. Rare, distinctive behaviours are pushed out to the edges. The edges are where you learn something about a particular offender.
  • Regions. Behaviours that share an underlying style cluster in the same area of the map. The software doesn't label those areas. An analyst looks at the plot and decides where to draw the lines, which is why critics call this step subjective.

Try moving the sample slider down to 20 cases. The map gets noisy and the themes blur, because small samples give unstable association scores. Real studies usually use at least a hundred offences for this reason.

The orientation of the map doesn't matter. Only the distances do, so a new sample may come out rotated or mirrored.

5

From behaviour to geography

A geographical profile takes the locations of a crime series and estimates where the offender probably lives. It works on the idea that offenders commit most crimes fairly near home, but not right on their doorstep. The catch is that you have to feed it the right crimes. If burglaries by a different person get mixed in, the estimate drifts.

6

The limits

Real data is messier than this simulation. Police files are written for prosecution, not research, so behaviours are often missing or inconsistently recorded. Real offenders also mix styles more than simulated ones, and the regions in published studies are rarely as clean as the ones here.

SSA describes patterns across many offences. It doesn't tell you with certainty which style a single offender has. Evidence that behavioural style predicts how far offenders travel or where they live is mixed. In practice, SSA is best treated as a way of exploring data and generating hypotheses. The geographic part, estimating a likely home area, is done with separate spatial methods such as Rossmo's criminal geographic targeting.

All data on this page is simulated for teaching. The geographic model is a simplified version of a distance-decay function with a buffer zone, and the hit score is the percentage of the map searched before reaching the true home, where lower is better.
